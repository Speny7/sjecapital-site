Your pay before anything is taken off: the figure on your contract or job offer.

Where to find it
Where to find itOn your payslip, your P60, or in the HMRC app. Most people are on 1257L, which means you can earn £12,570 before paying Income Tax. A different number means HMRC has adjusted your tax-free amount, for example for a company car or an underpayment from last year. Codes starting with S are Scottish; a K code means you owe extra.

Most employees are put into a pension automatically once they earn over £10,000 a year.

Where to find itLook for a pension line on your payslip. If there is one, choose Yes. The percentages are usually on your pension provider's welcome letter or app.

Of your salary. Minimum 5%.

Which one do I have?
Standard workplace pension (most people): your contribution comes out of your pay and you get Income Tax relief on it, but you still pay National Insurance on your full salary.

Salary sacrifice: you agree to a slightly lower salary and your employer pays the difference into your pension for you. Because your salary is lower, both you and your employer pay less National Insurance, so you end up with a bit more in your pocket. Not sure which you have? If the gross pay on your payslip is lower than your contracted salary, it is probably salary sacrifice. Otherwise choose Standard.

Leave at 0 if you do not have a company car. The P11D value is what the car cost new, including VAT, delivery and any optional extras, but not the first registration fee or road tax. It is not what your employer actually paid for it.

Where to find itYour employer's P11D form (they give you one each July), the car's original order form, or search the make, model and trim on the manufacturer's website or a site like Parkers.
More info

HMRC taxes you on a percentage of the car's value every year, as if it were extra salary. The percentage depends on the car's CO2 emissions: fully electric cars are 4% in 2026/27; plug-in hybrids are 4% to 16% depending on their electric-only range; petrol and diesel cars are 17% to 37% depending on CO2, with 4% more for older diesels that do not meet the RDE2 standard (still capped at 37%).

Where to find itYour P11D form shows the exact percentage. Enter it above; 4% is the rate for a fully electric car.

Anything your employer gives you that is not cash, such as private medical insurance, a gym membership or a fuel card. Leave at 0 if none.

Where to find itThe taxable value of each benefit is listed on your P11D form. Add them together and enter the total.
More info

Income outside this job, such as a second job, a pension in payment or rent from a property. HMRC taxes it alongside your salary, so it can push your salary into a higher band and counts towards the Child Benefit charge. We show only the extra tax your salary carries as a result; the tax on the other income itself is collected separately. Leave at 0 if none.

More info

Child Benefit is a payment for parents of children under 16 (or under 20 if still in education). If you or your partner earn over £60,000 a year, some of it is taken back through tax, and all of it once you earn £80,000. Only the higher earner's income counts. Pension contributions reduce the income that is counted.

More info

Scotland sets its own Income Tax bands. Choose Scotland if your tax code starts with an S. National Insurance is the same everywhere.

Assumptions
  • Tax year 2026/27. Other income is taxed alongside your salary; we show the extra tax it causes on your salary, not the tax on the other income itself. Tax codes are applied as a simple allowance; HMRC uses them slightly differently in payroll, so pennies may differ.
  • Pension percentages apply to your whole basic salary, as most schemes do. Some schemes use a narrower band of earnings.
  • NI is worked out on an annual basis; a bonus paid in one month can attract slightly different NI for monthly-paid employees.
  • Benefits in kind carry Income Tax for you and 15% Class 1A NI for the employer, but no employee NI.
  • Figures are worked to the penny: each amount is rounded to the nearest penny as it is calculated, and every later figure is built from those amounts, so every line adds up exactly. HMRC's own systems may round some figures down to whole pounds, which can differ by a few pence. Illustrative, not advice.
