Is a company car worth it for a director in 2026/27?

By Spencer Elbert · 17 November 2026 · 6 minute read ·

Electric cars have changed the answer. Here is when a company car saves a director money, when running your own car wins, and the sums behind both, for 2026/27.

The short version

  • A company car is a benefit in kind: you pay Income Tax on the list price × a percentage set by its CO2 emissions.
  • Electric cars are taxed at just 4% in 2026/27; petrol and diesel cars at up to 37%.
  • On a £45,000 electric car leased for £550 a month, a higher rate director is £2,880.17 a year better off with a company car.
  • A £35,000 petrol car on the same terms costs £1,734.48 a year more than running your own.
  • New electric cars bought before 31 March 2027 can be deducted in full in the first year.
  • Drive a lot for business? Your own car with 55p a mile can win.

A company car used to be one of the classic director perks. Then the tax rules made most petrol and diesel company cars a poor deal. Electric cars have changed the answer again: for many directors, a company electric car is now one of the most tax-efficient ways to run a car. This guide shows why, with the sums.

A like-for-like comparison. Both options are measured in the same after-tax money: how much less you end up with personally each year. The company’s Corporation Tax relief is already counted, because every pound it spends on the car is a pound it cannot pay you as a dividend.

How company car tax works

If your company provides a car you can use privately, including for commuting, HMRC treats it as a benefit in kind. The taxable value is the car's P11D value (its list price when new, including VAT and optional extras) multiplied by a percentage set by its CO2 emissions. You pay Income Tax on that value, and the company pays Class 1A National Insurance of 15% on it.

For 2026/27 the percentages range from 4% for fully electric cars to 37% for cars emitting 155g/km or more. Plug-in hybrids emitting 50g/km or less are taxed on their electric range, from 4% for 130 miles or more to 16% for under 30 miles. Diesel cars that do not meet the RDE2 standard pay an extra 4%, up to the 37% maximum.

An electric car: why the answer is often yes

Take an electric car with a P11D value of £45,000, leased by the company for £550 a month, with £1,200 a year of insurance and servicing. The director is a higher rate taxpayer, and the company pays Corporation Tax at 19%.

Plug-in hybrids

A plug-in hybrid with 45 miles of electric range falls in the 10% band for 2026/27. On the same £45,000 example it still comes out ahead of running your own car, but by less. Be aware that from 2028/29, all plug-in hybrids emitting 50g/km or less are due to move to a single 18% rate, whatever their electric range, which will change the sums for anyone choosing a hybrid on a long lease.

Buying rather than leasing

Most directors lease, and the company deducts the lease payments from its profits as it pays them. If the company buys the car instead, outright or on hire purchase, the tax relief works differently, and the date you buy matters.

Bought new before 31 March 2027: all the relief in year one

A new, unused, fully electric car qualifies for a 100% first-year allowance: the company deducts the whole cost from its profits in the year it buys the car. For a £45,000 electric car, that saves Corporation Tax of:

Unless the government extends the allowance again, a new electric car bought from 1 April 2027 goes into what HMRC calls the company’s main pool. What stays the same

  • Leased cars are not affected. The lease payments are still deducted as they are paid, before or after the date.
  • Your company car tax does not change. You pay the benefit-in-kind tax, and the company pays Class 1A, whether the car is bought or leased.
  • Other cars: petrol, diesel and hybrid cars never qualify for the 100% allowance. Those emitting 50g/km or less get the main pool rate, 14% a year now and 12% from 1 April 2027; those above 50g/km get 6% a year.
  • The new 50% first-year allowance does not help. The Autumn Budget 2026 brought in a 50% allowance for equipment from 1 January 2027, but it does not cover cars.
  • Selling the car later: the sale price is added back to the company’s profits, so the relief is not free money. Getting it early mainly helps cash flow.

Whether buying beats leasing depends on how long you keep the car and what it sells for, so it is worth running the numbers for your own situation.

Mileage: when your own car wins

If you run your own car, the company can pay you 55p a mile tax-free for the first 10,000 business miles in the tax year, and 25p a mile after that. For 10,000 business miles, that is 10,000 × 55p = , which the company can also deduct. Directors who drive a lot for work, in a car with modest costs, can find their own car is the better option, especially if the company car would be petrol or diesel.

Things to watch

  • Private fuel. If the company pays for fuel for private journeys in a petrol, diesel or hybrid car, there is an extra fuel benefit charge of £29,200 × the car's percentage, whatever the fuel actually costs. It rarely pays. Electricity for an electric car is not treated as fuel.
  • Rates are rising. The company car percentages go up over the next few years:
    • Electric cars: 4% in 2026/27, 5% in 2027/28, 7% in 2028/29, 9% in 2029/30 and 10% in 2030/31.
    • Everything else, from 2030/31: the Autumn Budget 2026 adds 1 percentage point to every other band. Plug-in hybrids emitting 1 to 50g/km go from 19% to 20%, and petrol and diesel cars each go up 1 point, with the highest band capped at 40%.
    • What that means: the £45,000 electric car’s taxable benefit rises from £1,800 today to £45,000 × 10% = in 2030/31. Electric cars still stay far cheaper than petrol or diesel.
  • A new mileage charge. From April 2028, Electric Vehicle Excise Duty adds a pay-per-mile charge for electric and plug-in hybrid cars. It applies to company cars and personal cars alike.
  • Reporting, now through payroll. For 2026/27, the company reports the car to HMRC on a P11D form by 6 July 2027 and pays the Class 1A National Insurance by 22 July 2027 (if paying electronically), unless it has registered to payroll the benefit. From 6 April 2027, company cars must be reported through payroll: the car’s taxable value is added to your payslip each month and the Income Tax on it comes off through PAYE, instead of being collected later through your tax code or a P11D. This was confirmed in the Autumn Budget 2026.

Common questions

Is a company car worth it for a director?

For electric cars, often yes. On a £45,000 electric car leased for £550 a month, a higher rate director is £2,880.17 a year better off than running the same car personally. Petrol and diesel company cars usually cost more than running your own car.

How much tax will I pay on an electric company car?

The taxable benefit is 4% of the car's list price in 2026/27. On a £45,000 car that is £1,800.00, so a higher rate taxpayer pays £1,800.00 × 40% = a year, or £60.00 a month.

What is a P11D value?

The car's list price when new, including VAT, delivery and optional extras, but not the first registration fee or road tax. It is shown on company car lease quotes and manufacturer price lists.

Does the company pay National Insurance on a company car?

Yes. The company pays Class 1A National Insurance of 15% on the taxable benefit. On a £1,800.00 benefit, that is £1,800.00 × 15% = , which the company can deduct for Corporation Tax.

Can my company claim the full cost of an electric car?

Only if it buys a new, unused, fully electric car before 31 March 2027: it can then deduct the whole cost from its profits that year through a 100% first-year allowance. Bought from 1 April 2027, the cost is relieved at 12% of the remaining balance each year instead. Leased cars work differently: the lease payments are deducted as they are paid.
